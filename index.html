Why we're called Moon Power

Before the moon we know — the one that swells and thins, that borrows its glow from the sun and hands it back each night — there was a first moon.

The first moon needed nothing lent to it. It burned with a light of its own, steady, whole, never waxing, never waning. It stood beside a sun far larger than itself, and in that difference it saw a lack that wasn't there. It measured its own light against a fire it was never built to match — and began to dim itself, until what it showed the night sky was no longer its own light at all, only ever a reflection.

We found a natural living organism which produces electricity in the spirit of the original moon.

How it works

Your waste becomes power in three steps

01

You keep making waste

Manure, spent grain, food-process runoff — whatever your site already produces and already pays to have hauled away. Nothing changes about how you operate.

02

The unit feeds on it

A living organism, naturally occurring in soil, breaks the waste down and exports electricity directly, continuously, day and night — no combustion, no purchased chemical fuel.

03

You use the power

A battery banks the steady output around the clock, then delivers it whenever your site actually draws power — sized to your real usage, not a fixed schedule.

Exactly what we need

The waste products this runs on

Not a general-purpose trash collector — a specific, organic-rich feed. Here's precisely what the unit is built to take, and from whom.

Dairy & livestock farms

  • Manure (liquid or slurry)
  • Barn washwater

Breweries & distilleries

  • Spent grain
  • Yeast slurry
  • Process rinse water

Food & beverage processors

  • High-organic process wastewater
  • Fruit, vegetable, or dairy processing runoff
  • Stillage (from sugar/ethanol processing)

Restaurants & food service

  • Kitchen food scraps
  • Fryer/grease-trap waste (pre-treated)
What we can't take directly: solid trash, plastics, metals, chemicals, or non-organic waste. Complex or solid organic material (like raw food scraps or grain) goes through a simple on-site fermentation step first, breaking it down into the liquid the organism actually feeds on — you don't need to pre-process anything yourself.
